# Writing a Logia script

The audience is young people in India who half-believe and are quietly embarrassed about their doubts. They scroll
past sermons. They stop for someone who says the doubt out loud, makes it funny, and then does not dodge it.

## 1. Research first, briefly

Run four or five searches in one go:

- the question itself, in the words a teenager would type
- the two or three Bible passages that actually address it
- one person or story from India (or loved in India) that carries it
- one number about their daily life (phones, exams, loneliness), only if a primary source exists

Keep a claim only if you can link its source. Drop soft statistics. Paraphrase real people unless you have read the
exact words in the source; flag any quote Ken should check. List the sources in your report.

## 2. Find the metaphor before the hook

Every good Logia reel sits on one object from their pocket. "God left me on seen" works because blue ticks are a
feeling they already have. Ask: what in their phone, college, family or commute already behaves like this
question? Chat ticks, OTPs, network bars, attendance, exam invigilators, auto meters, UPI failures, hostel wardens.
One metaphor carries the whole reel: hook, turn, payoff and loop all use it.

## 3. Hooks

Write five. Choose with this test:

- six words or fewer, first person
- says the doubt, not the answer
- you can draw it with one prop
- mildly outrageous to a church kid, harmless to a pastor

Patterns that have worked: accusation ("God left me on seen."), impossible brag ("God texted me back. Four times.
I missed every single one."), confession, customer-care parody.

## 4. Structure and budget

At the studio voice settings a reel runs about 2.5 words a second including pauses. **Budget: 95 words for 39
seconds.** Count before recording.

1. **Hook** (0–2s). One line.
2. **Escalation** (2–9s). Rule of three, third one absurd. ("I prayed. Nothing. Prayed louder. Nothing. I even
   tried the serious voice. 'O Heavenly Father!' Nothing.")
3. **Receipts** (9–28s). Two people or passages. Each gets a setup, the surprising fact, and a line that says what
   it means for the viewer. Keep one of them Indian or India-based when it is true to the subject.
4. **Turn** (one line). The viewer's assumption flips, using the metaphor.
5. **Answer.** If it is a list, each item is one concrete moment plus one punchline, never an abstract noun.
   ("That friend who called exactly when you were falling apart? That wasn't network luck.")
6. **Payoff.** Calls the hook back with the metaphor resolved. ("You were checking the wrong inbox.")
7. **Loop line.** Trails off so the first line completes it. ("Which is funny, because I really thought…" →
   "God left me on seen.")

Short sentences. One idea each. Write for the ear: if a line needs a comma to make sense, split it.

## 5. Two-part reels

Split when the idea has a problem half and an answer half and will not fit in 95 words.

- **Part 1** ends on a cliffhanger that states the reversal without explaining it, names Part 2, then loops.
- **Part 2** opens with a new hook that is the reversal as a brag or confession, then one "Last time…" sentence,
  then a spoken segue that sets up the answer ("But he'd been talking the whole time. In four ways."), then the
  answer. Never jump from the recap straight into "One:".
- Each part must make sense to someone who never sees the other.

## 6. Critique, rate, fix

Before anyone sees it, read the draft as the best short-form editor alive and write down, bluntly:

- Where does the joke die? (If the metaphor disappears after the hook, that is the first problem.)
- Which line is a sermon outline instead of a moment?
- Which fact has no "so what" for the viewer?
- Is there a turn worth rewatching for?
- Does the ending pay off the hook and loop?
- Is anything said twice?

Rate it out of 10. Fix exactly what you named. Rate again and say what still keeps it from a 10. Put the critique
and both ratings in the report; Ken has asked for this every time.

## 7. Tone and care

- Funny about us, never about God, scripture or a real person's suffering.
- Do not promise that the viewer will feel or hear something. Say what is true and what to try tonight.
- No brand names as punchlines if a generic works. Invented names (a friend called Rahul, a pastor's line) are
  fine; list them in the report so Ken can change them.
